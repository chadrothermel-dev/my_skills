## American Roots: Spirituals, Gospel, Blues, Jazz, Country & Folk

### Spirituals, Gospel & Early Black Music
- **Spirituals** arose among enslaved African Americans, blending West African musical practices (call and response, polyrhythm, improvisation) with Christian hymnody. The **Fisk Jubilee Singers** (touring from 1871) brought them to concert audiences worldwide.
- **Ragtime** (c. 1895–1918): syncopated piano music, led by **Scott Joplin** ("Maple Leaf Rag," 1899).
- **Gospel:** **Thomas A. Dorsey** (often called "the father of gospel music") fused blues with sacred song in Chicago in the 1930s. Mahalia Jackson became its great voice, and **Sister Rosetta Tharpe** brought electric guitar to gospel, influencing early rock 'n' roll.
- Explain **minstrelsy** as a harmful 19th-century entertainment built on racist caricature that nonetheless shaped American popular music and the music business.

### Blues
| Stream | Hallmarks | Key figures |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Delta and country blues** (1920s–30s) | Solo voice and guitar; slide guitar; raw, personal lyrics | Charley Patton, Son House, Skip James, Robert Johnson (recorded 1936–37) |
| **Classic women's blues** (1920s) | Vaudeville-style singers with bands; the first blues hits on record | Mamie Smith ("Crazy Blues," 1920), Ma Rainey, Bessie Smith |
| **Texas and Piedmont blues** | Fluid single-note lines (Texas); ragtime-influenced fingerpicking (Piedmont) | Blind Lemon Jefferson, T-Bone Walker, Blind Willie McTell |
| **Chicago electric blues** (1940s–50s) | Amplified guitar and harmonica, full band; the Chess Records sound | Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, Little Walter, Willie Dixon |
| **Later blues and revival** | Blues rock, British blues revival, soul blues | B.B. King, Buddy Guy, Etta James; the British bands who reintroduced American blues to white American audiences |

W.C. Handy, who published "St. Louis Blues" in 1914, is often called "the Father of the Blues" for popularizing the form in sheet music.

### Jazz
| Era (approximate) | Hallmarks | Key figures |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **New Orleans / early jazz** (1910s–20s) | Collective improvisation; brass bands; ragtime and blues roots | Buddy Bolden, King Oliver, Jelly Roll Morton, Louis Armstrong (Hot Five and Hot Seven, 1925–28, made the soloist central) |
| **Swing / big band** (1930s–mid-40s) | Dance-band arrangements, riffs, swing feel; jazz as America's popular music | Duke Ellington, Count Basie, Benny Goodman, Billie Holiday, Ella Fitzgerald |
| **Bebop** (1940s) | Fast tempos, complex harmony; jazz as art music for listening | Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, Thelonious Monk, Bud Powell |
| **Cool, hard bop, modal** (1950s) | Relaxed tone (cool); blues and gospel feel (hard bop); improvising on scales (modal) | Miles Davis (*Kind of Blue*, 1959), Art Blakey, Horace Silver, Dave Brubeck, John Coltrane |
| **Free and avant-garde** (late 1950s–60s) | Loosening or dropping fixed harmony and form | Ornette Coleman, late Coltrane, Cecil Taylor, Sun Ra |
| **Latin jazz and bossa nova** | Afro-Cuban rhythms; Brazilian bossa nova crossover | Machito, Mario Bauzá, Dizzy Gillespie with Chano Pozo, Stan Getz and João Gilberto |
| **Fusion** (late 1960s–70s) | Rock and funk rhythms, electric instruments | Miles Davis (*Bitches Brew*, 1970), Weather Report, Herbie Hancock, Return to Forever |
| **Contemporary** | Neo-traditionalism, jazz meets hip-hop and R&B, spiritual jazz revival | Wynton Marsalis, Esperanza Spalding, Robert Glasper, Kamasi Washington |

The first jazz record (1917) was made by a white New Orleans group, the Original Dixieland Jass Band, a well-known example of who got to record first versus who created the music.

### Country
- **Roots:** British Isles ballads and fiddle tunes, African American banjo (an instrument with West African origins) and string-band music, gospel, and the blues.
- **The 1927 Bristol Sessions** (Tennessee/Virginia) recorded the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers and are often called "the big bang of country music." The **Grand Ole Opry** radio show began in 1925.
- **Styles:**
  - Western swing (Bob Wills).
  - Honky-tonk (Hank Williams).
  - The Nashville Sound (Chet Atkins, Patsy Cline).
  - Bakersfield (Buck Owens, Merle Haggard).
  - Outlaw country (Willie Nelson, Waylon Jennings).
  - Country pop, alt-country, and Americana.
- **Black artists in country history:** DeFord Bailey (an early Opry star), Charley Pride, and contemporary artists reclaiming the genre's roots.

### Folk & Bluegrass
- **Bluegrass:** Bill Monroe ("the father of bluegrass") and his Blue Grass Boys, with Flatt & Scruggs, shaped it in the 1940s: fast tempos, banjo rolls, high harmonies.
- **The folk revival:** Lead Belly, Woody Guthrie, and Pete Seeger; Harry Smith's *Anthology of American Folk Music* (1952). The 1960s brought Joan Baez and Bob Dylan, whose electric set at the 1965 Newport Folk Festival sparked folk rock.
